Can a world model tell when a robot pick goes wrong?

By Janelle Ghanem

Amazon's warehouse robots move one item at a time from a yellow tote to a bin. Every so often the box tears open or the item comes apart. I built a harness that watches each pick frame by frame, like a live safety monitor would, and checks whether a model raises the alarm when the damage starts.

Labels still help when it counts

A small world model that only saw normal picks ranks failures about as well as a small supervised model (AUROC 0.68 vs 0.70, gap not significant). As a live alarm at 5% false alarms, the supervised one catches more: 33% vs 21%.

Amazon's offline model is far ahead

Watching the whole clip after the fact, Amazon's classifier catches 73% of failures at 13% false alarms (the ◆). The best live monitor here catches 45% at that rate.

Torn packaging is the hard case

Every monitor is near a coin flip on boxes that open. Items coming apart are easier to spot, for Amazon's model too.

Something shows up before the damage

Failed picks already score higher than normal ones before any damage is visible (AUROC 0.64), and early alarms fire 2–3× more often than chance. A warning sign, a difference in what was picked, or late labels? Not settled: 81 clips is too few.

More than motion

Most of the world model's score tracks how much the scene moves, but taking motion out costs almost nothing (AUROC 0.68 → 0.67). What flags failures is something else.

V-JEPA2 still needs a GPU

It ran on just 30 clips on a laptop-class CPU, so its result can't be told apart from chance, in either direction.

How it works

Score every frame, not every clip

Amazon's own baseline looks at a whole clip afterwards. A monitor on a live robot has to decide while the video is still arriving, so everything here is judged that way.

Pull chosen videos out of a 254 GB archive without downloading all of it.

Score

On Amazon's official test split: did it alarm, and how soon after the damage started?

scripts/run_*.py

One catch along the way. The standard Hugging Face V-JEPA2 code lets the model peek at the future frames it's supposed to predict. This monitor feeds it the past frames on their own, so its predictions are honest.

Why it's hard

What a GPU run would answer

  1. Run V-JEPA2 on all 11,251 test picks at full resolution. Does a big pretrained world model beat the small one?
  2. Fine-tune it on normal picks only. Recent work says off-the-shelf world models "dream of success" and miss failures; this tests that on real warehouse data.
  3. Add NVIDIA Cosmos as a second world model, and Amazon's MViT-B classifier as a reference.
